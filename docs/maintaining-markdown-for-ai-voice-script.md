## Section 01 (Intro) — Script

Welcome. Before we start — imagine you're onboarding a new colleague. A freelancer, a consultant, whoever. You hand them a list of documents and tell them to read up.

Now: some of those documents were written a year ago and went out of date six months ago.

A human gets suspicious eventually. They notice something doesn't match, they ask someone, they self-correct.

Your AI never does. It reads that file fresh every time, with no way of knowing it's stale — and it will confidently act on it the hundredth time and the thousandth time, exactly as it did the first.

_(pause)_

Writing documentation used to be the expensive half. AI made it nearly free. Checking whether it's still true got more expensive, because now there's far more of it.

And that half doesn't get automated. It's nobody else's job.

Maintain markdown for AI. I'm Han Che — let's get into it.

---

## Delivery Constraints

- **Four engineered lines, keep verbatim:** the "your AI never does" contrast; "the hundredth time and the thousandth time"; the cost inversion; "it's nobody else's job."
- **"doesn't get automated", never "isn't automated yet."** The point is that it is judgment and will not be automated — not that it is waiting its turn.
- **Do not say "markdown" in the first thirty seconds.** The format word is earned after the problem is felt.
- **No hedging frame.** Not "I felt it was important" or "I wanted to have a short talk about". Go from the thesis straight to the title.
- **Say the name.** The recording is going to YouTube as a permanent reference.
- **Target 60–75 seconds.** Earlier takes ran to two minutes; the trims that fixed it were cutting the onboarding setup to just handing over the documents, and dropping the hedge.
- **No new visual for section 01.** A stickman illustration was considered and rejected: it costs the time just saved, splits attention during the one unmediated moment of the talk, and sets a register the later content has to climb out of. The title slide stays up throughout.

## Section 02 (Markdown in AI Workflows) — Script

> **Notice to Speaker:** This script is to be internalised, not read aloud verbatim. Reading a script verbatim is audible on a recording. Internalise the progression of beats, honor the delivery constraints, and speak naturally to the room.

Before we start, I want to establish why markdown files matter so much in AI workflows.

We're in the agentic era now — it isn't 2023 anymore. Agents aren't chatbots you copy-paste into. They read files on your machine, write files, call tools, search the web. Put those in a loop and they carry out real tasks, the way the new colleague you just onboarded would.

And like that colleague, an agent needs the right information at the right time. That information lives in markdown — which is just a plain text file.

Those files fall into three categories.

**Knowledge** — what the agent looks up on demand, when the task calls for it. You don't hand a new starter everything on day one.

**Instructions** — skills. How to do a release, step by step. Written once, called by name.

**And memory.** Not ChatGPT's memory — a file the agent reads at the start of every single conversation.

_(pause)_

And if that file is stale, or wrong, the agent reads that wrong information every single time. Confidently, and silently — without you ever noticing.

_(pause)_

And that's one document.

---

## Delivery Constraints — Section 02

- **The three category names get a beat of separation each**, not a list read at speed.
- **The pause before the memory closer is load-bearing.** That closer is the one line in this section the room should feel, and it is the bridge into section 03.
- **"Silently, without you ever noticing"** — keep this phrasing. It names the consequence rather than the mechanism, which is what makes it land.
- **Name "skills" explicitly**, in its own half-sentence rather than as an aside. The audience will meet the word constantly over the next year and needs to recognise it.
- **Keep the day-one anchor on the knowledge category.** Earlier takes dropped it and the category went abstract while the other two kept concrete images.
- **Do not explain markdown's features.** Headers, tables, checkboxes and frontmatter were all cut deliberately: half the room does not need them and the other half cannot use them yet. Frontmatter in particular belongs to section 05, where OKF makes it pay — introducing it here spends the audience's one first encounter in the wrong place.
- **Do not re-explain the onboarding analogy.** Section 01 earned it; here it is a one-clause callback only. Earlier takes lost thirty seconds restating it and starved the three categories.
- **Target 90 seconds.** First take ran to three minutes; the fat was in the middle, not the ends.
- **The closing line is the bridge into section 03.** It sets up the scale move without announcing it, so when the corpus side of the 03 chart arrives the audience does the multiplication themselves. "Document", not "file" — section 03's chart says document, and matching it makes the handoff feel like the same object continuing rather than a new one appearing.

And that's one document.

## Section 04 (Where the Effort Goes) — Scripts

> **Notice to Speaker:** This script is to be internalised, not read aloud verbatim. Reading a script verbatim is audible on a recording. Internalise the progression of beats, honor the delivery constraints, and speak naturally to the room.

### 04.1 — the boundaries

So the brake is gone. Which raises the obvious question: what does it actually take to check a document?

It's not one job. It's three, and they're not interchangeable.

On the left, the machine. Deterministic — same input, same answer, every time. Binary: it passes or it fails. Cheap, and fast. This is the part you want doing as much as possible.

On the right, the human. Not deterministic, slow, expensive. This is where the semantic work happens — is this actually true, is it still useful. And it's still where almost all trust comes from.

And in the middle, AI. It works in natural language, so it sits on the human side of that line — not deterministic. It costs tokens. What it can and can't be trusted with is genuinely unsettled.

_(pause)_

Left to right. Cheap and certain, to expensive and human. Let's take them in order.

### 04.2 — machine

Start with the low-hanging fruit. What can a machine check, every time, for free?

Here's a markdown file — an onboarding process. At the top, the frontmatter: key-value pairs in YAML that carry metadata, mostly for the machine and the agent. Underneath, the body.

A machine can check the shape of that metadata. Is the type declared. Is the date a real date. Is the link well-formed — and it can even follow it and see if it's still there.

And that's the ceiling. It can tell you the field exists. It can't tell you whether what's written in it is true.

### 04.3 — AI

So what does AI add?

It can follow that reference. Read the document on the other end. And notice that this one says five days and that one says seven.

That's a contradiction it found on its own — and what it does with it matters more than finding it. It can flag that one section and raise a ticket, so a human resolves a single line instead of re-reading the document.

Those are signals. Every signal narrows what a human actually has to look at.

_(pause)_

But can it make the call? It can produce one. It's like stopping someone in the street, handing them both documents, and asking which is right. You'll get an answer.

If you own this process — would you trust that person?

### 04.4 — human

Which leaves this.

Is the coffee thing still how the team works. Is this person still the owner.

No amount of tooling answers those. You have to know — and you're the one accountable when it's wrong.

_(pause)_

The machine narrows it. The AI narrows it further. What's left is the part that was always yours.

## Delivery Constraints — Section 04

- **The seam between 04.3 and 04.4 is the hardest joint in the talk and the one that failed in four consecutive takes.** The street analogy ends on a question and nothing answers it on that slide. Ask it, stop, change slide. The human column is the answer.
- **Say nothing about human judgment before slide 04.4.** Every rehearsal leaked it into 04.3, which left the final slide with nothing to deliver but a recap.
- **"What's left is the part that was always yours"** — not "that will never change". The banked phrasing makes the same claim without predicting the future, which is the version a skeptic cannot argue with. It is also the second time the audience meets this claim and the first time it has been argued, so it carries section 07.
- **Two frontmatter examples, not six.** Early takes enumerated type, valid values, ISO date, staleness comparison, URL form, link liveness and H1 structure. The audience needs to believe machines can check shape, not to see the list. The enumeration cost roughly forty seconds.
- **Define non-deterministic once**, on 04.1 when the axis is introduced. Do not redefine it for the AI column.
- **The ticket example belongs on 04.3, before the analogy**, as the positive case. It is the most concrete illustration of "signals narrow the work".
- **Do not close on a comparison.** "A much better workflow than before AI" and "information a human still needs to verify" both appeared in rehearsal and both are descriptions where the script has a claim.
- **No hedges on the judgment claim.** "Usually", "most likely", "in most cases", "I would say", "probably not" all appeared. The format is recorded with no Q&A, so a hedge does not invite dialogue — it just lowers the claim.
- **Target three minutes across four slides.** Rehearsal runs came in at 4:30, 4:00 and 3:45. The left-hand diagram is fixed across all four slides, so only the delta gets narrated after 04.1.

## Section 05 (Google OKF) — Script

The machine finds structure. The AI adds a first layer of meaning, to help a human decide faster. And what's left was always yours.

So let's go back to the machine half, and ask how we make it bigger.

A machine works on structure. Which means it only works if we agree on a shared vocabulary.

This is Google's Open Knowledge Format. It's open, and it's early — version 0.2, still moving.

It names the fields, and says what each one means. Some you've already seen — type, title, description, sources. And the interesting ones: trust, and lifecycle. That a document was generated by an agent, and verified by a human. And `stale_after` — freshness.

_(pause)_

If we all agree to use the same vocabulary, the checker gets written once. And it works everywhere.

Which makes the machine's half a lot bigger than checking that a date is a date.

## Delivery Constraints — Section 05

- **"Generated by an agent, verified by a human" is the line to keep.** It arrived in rehearsal, not in the draft. It is the first moment in the talk where provenance becomes concrete rather than abstract, and it is what makes the trust family feel real.
- **Say the version number.** Early and still moving is honest, and it turns the slide into an invitation to look rather than a recommendation to adopt blind.
- **Do not say "design by contract".** It appeared in rehearsal as a second term reached for when the first felt unlanded. Standardization is the word this audience has, and the second term costs five seconds for nothing.
- **Say "structure" once.** A rehearsal take used it three times in one breath — machine works on structure, works better with shared vocabulary, which is structure — and the word went muddy.
- **The last line is the handoff into 06 and must not resolve.** It points at the machine column growing without naming a tool. Earlier drafts closed on "so someone should write it", which was rejected as too much of a drumroll — it pre-announces the reveal. The callback to the section 04.2 ceiling ("checking that a date is a date") does the same work quietly, because the audience already met that example as the limit.
- **Do not describe OKF as "governing frontmatter for AI usage".** That names what it is for, not what it does. It names the fields and says what each one means.
- **Target 65 seconds.** This is a short section by design — it is the first thing in the talk that is an answer rather than a problem, and it only has to earn one idea.

## Section 06 (Steering the AI) — Scripts

> **Notice to Speaker:** This script is to be internalised, not read aloud verbatim. Reading a script verbatim is audible on a recording. Internalise the progression of beats, honor the delivery constraints, and speak naturally to the room.

### 06.1 — Intro to markdown-harness

So the goal is to make the machine's half do more. And this is what I've been building, in my own time, over the past couple of weeks. I call it markdown-harness.

The idea is one sentence: **a document can tell you how much of itself to believe.**

It's not about generating trust. Trust is semantic — a machine can never produce it. What it can do is raise the floor. Reliable signals, that tell an agent or a human that something needs attention.

You've seen these three circles before. Same three, different job — this time it's what the tool does to them.

Deterministic, cheap, fast signals, handed to the AI while it's working. The machine column reaching into the AI column. That's the arrow.

_(pause)_

And the human side is greyed out on purpose. Not because it's solved. Because this doesn't go there. That part was always yours.

### 06.2 — Deterministic core

Three ways in. Three ways out. And the middle is the same every time.

Whatever starts it, the core does one thing: read the configuration, run the logic. Same input, same answer. It's fully deterministic.

The one I want to show you is the first. The agent goes to read a file — and before it does, it's told that file is stale.

The other two, briefly. On commit: a check that the frontmatter meets the standard. It passes, or it blocks.

And on a schedule: a nightly run that regenerates what can be regenerated, and raises a ticket on what can't.

Let me show you the first one.

## Delivery Constraints — Section 06

- **Say the line.** "A document can tell you how much of itself to believe" is on the slide and was never once said aloud across a full rehearsal session. Every paraphrase reached for instead — "what the document can tell about itself" — is weaker than the sentence already written.
- **Name the diagram as a callback.** The three circles are the section 04 shape with different semantics. Unnamed, the audience tries to reconcile two diagrams and stops listening.
- **Name the grey circle, and say why.** The rehearsal take that worked said the tool deliberately does not reach the human side. Leaving it unsaid makes the picture argue against the talk's thesis — greyed out reads as solved.
- **Do not lead with "100% deterministic".** The plain-language version comes first — three ways in, three ways out, the middle is the same every time — and the word lands afterwards as a label. Leading with the word spends it on an audience that doesn't have it yet.
- **Cut "pretty much self-explanatory" and "pretty simplistic in a sense".** Both appeared in rehearsal. They are hedges, and they tell the audience not to look at the slide.
- **Keep the runtime example short.** Rehearsal versions carried steering, staleness, injection, user warning and ticket creation in one breath — for a mechanism that is about to be demonstrated live. The shape is enough: the agent goes to read a file, and before it does, it's told the file is stale.
- **Commit trigger: pick one framing.** Rehearsal described it as both a gate that blocks a commit and a loop where the agent reads the failure and fixes it. The non-technical half of the room hears a gate; say only that.
- **"In my own time, over the past couple of weeks" stays.** It sets the register correctly — this is work shown, not a product pitched.
- **Target 100 seconds across both slides**, leaving the demo its full five minutes.

## Section 06.3 (Live Demo) — Narration Beats (Not a Script)

> **Notice to Speaker:** This slide has no verbatim script. A demo narrated word-for-word breaks if the machine behaves unexpectedly. Deliver the fixed set-up line before switching to the terminal, then navigate the beats naturally in sequence.

### Fixed Set-Up Line (deliver before switching to terminal)

> Three panels. Same question, same document. The only difference is what each one is allowed to know.

### Narration Beats (in order, not verbatim)

1. **Before the run** — Deliver the set-up line above. Give the room a difference to watch for rather than expecting them to decipher terminal text.
2. **While it runs** — Name what the un-hooked panel is doing: answering confidently, from a document that went out of date. Nothing on screen says that document is stale, so say it aloud.
3. **The un-hooked result** — If it happened to notice the date, say so plainly and make it the point: sometimes it notices. It depends on the model and on whether it bothered to read the frontmatter. That is the argument, not an inconvenience.
4. **The injected panel** — The pirate answer is the proof the injection landed. Say what it demonstrates: the tool decided what the agent knew.
5. **The deny panel** — The clearest of the three for a non-technical room, because the outcome needs no reading. The read was refused and the agent says so.
6. **The log** — Deterministic detection, recorded, independent of what any model said.
7. **The config and the document** — After the run, never before. Effect first, then cause.
8. **The injection finding** — Say it out loud. The model correctly spotted an injected instruction and reported ignoring it. It is unsolved, and saying so is the credible version of the open call this talk is making.

## Delivery Constraints — Section 06.3

- **Record a backup run and cue it.** The talk is recorded with no live Q&A, so there is no prize for running live. A recorded run can be narrated calmly, has no dead seconds, and cannot fail. Running live with the recording cued is the compromise; running live with no backup is not.
- **Measured length: about 3 minutes 30 for the recorded version, against a 5 minute budget.** The remaining ninety seconds is the deck's only slack and should stay unspent here.
- **Cut the raw command output.** It was under consideration as a time-permitting extra. It is the third explanation of one mechanism: the technical half already has it and the rest will disengage. Decide it out now rather than at the podium.
- **Do not claim the un-hooked panel will miss the staleness.** It may catch it. The claim is that it might, and the hooked one always will.
- **The pirate line is evidence, not comedy.** Land the real finding first — what the un-hooked agent did with a stale document — and let the pirate answer be the proof the mechanism fired. Leading with the joke invites the room to file the whole thing as a toy.
